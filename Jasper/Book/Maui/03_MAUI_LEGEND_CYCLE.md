# Māui-tikitiki-a-Taranga: Māori narrative cycle

**Tradition note:** These are summaries of published Māori traditions, not one official script. Different iwi and narrators preserve different details. Specific variant details below are marked where possible.

## 1. A birth between loss and survival

Māui's name often appears as Māui-tikitiki-a-Taranga. In a well-known account, his mother Taranga wraps an apparently stillborn or miscarried baby in her tikitiki (topknot of hair) and casts him into the sea; the child survives and is raised by an ancestor/grandfather. Te Ara names Tamanui-ki-te-rangi in one retelling. The birth story is not simply a whimsical 'abandoned baby' trope: it carries ideas of grief, the sea, kinship and survival. Do not turn a community's account of stillbirth into a cheap joke.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/artwork/27618/taranga and https://teara.govt.nz/en/artwork/27619/maui-and-his-grandfather

## 2. Returning to his family

Māui finds Taranga and discovers his elder brothers. An older published account describes the brothers' similar names, his hiding of his mother's clothing to learn where she travels, following her underground in the form of a bird, and meeting his father Makeatutara. An error during a ritual connected to his father shapes his later fate in that version. These precise beats come from an older 1966 encyclopedia page based on collected texts; check more recent and iwi-specific tellings before adapting them.

Te Ara older source: https://teara.govt.nz/en/1966/maui-legends-of/page-2

## 3. The jawbone of Murirangawhenua

His ancestress Murirangawhenua gives him an extraordinary jawbone, which serves as a tool of inherited power and becomes central to the sun and fish stories. The gift is not equivalent to a generic wizard wand. The elder's knowledge, kinship and consent make the story possible; Māui's cleverness is not solitary.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/kaumatua-maori-elders/page-2
Waitangi Tribunal witnesses: https://waitangitribunal.govt.nz/en/inquiries/kaupapa-inquiries/mana-wahine/te-kete-puputu/briefs-of-evidence-2/who-are-our-atua-whaea-and-what-are-their-characteristics-and-stories/key-evidence-about-atua-whaea-2

## 4. Māui slows the sun

Tamanuiterā moves so fast that people cannot finish their tasks. Māui organizes his brothers and woven flax ropes, prepares, approaches the sun's rising place and restrains it with the aid of his ancestral jawbone. The result is longer days. Two story engines coexist: **collective labor** (brothers, rope-making and preparation) and **the danger of trying to command a cosmic being**.

An education-resource retelling by Wiremu Grace presents a fuller sequence, including a karakia. This dossier intentionally does **not** reproduce that sacred/ritual wording. Link readers to the actual author if study of the source is needed:
https://eng.mataurangamaori.tki.org.nz/Support-materials/Te-Reo-Maori/Maori-Myths-Legends-and-Contemporary-Stories/How-Maui-slowed-the-sun

For fiction, allow Maui to tell a *personal, invented reflection* on learning to ask his companions for help; do not reprint or manufacture the traditional karakia.

## 5. The great fish: Te Ika-a-Māui

While fishing with his brothers, Māui uses his jawbone hook and pulls up a huge fish that becomes the North Island. The South Island is named Te Waka-a-Māui in the famous naming tradition, and Rakiura is identified with the anchor stone. Other details—bait, an argument with siblings, carving the fish, the precise coast—vary and should be tied to a particular source. The fish episode is a story about place, ancestral identity and consequence, not merely a superpowered fishing record.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/first-peoples-in-maori-tradition and https://teara.govt.nz/en/first-peoples-in-maori-tradition/print

## 6. Mahuika's fire

Māui extinguishes household fires and approaches Mahuika, an ancestress associated with fire. She gives him fire from her nails, repeatedly; Māui wastes or extinguishes the gifts and returns until she is enraged. Fire spreads dangerously; Māui escapes by a bird transformation in some accounts and calls for rain. Fire remains in certain trees, allowing people to create it. The story presents ingenuity but also **disrespect, danger and the elder's power**. A romantic hero should not boast about this episode as if no one was harmed.

Te Ara historical account: https://teara.govt.nz/en/1966/maui-legends-of/page-5
Waitangi Tribunal: https://waitangitribunal.govt.nz/en/inquiries/kaupapa-inquiries/mana-wahine/te-kete-puputu/briefs-of-evidence-2/who-are-our-atua-whaea-and-what-are-their-characteristics-and-stories/key-evidence-about-atua-whaea-2

## 7. Māui and death: Hine-nui-te-pō

Seeking to end human mortality, Māui attempts to overcome the great ancestress Hine-nui-te-pō by entering her body in reverse and emerging through her mouth. In well-known accounts a watching small bird laughs or makes a sound, she wakes and Māui is crushed. Sources differ on whether the watcher is a fantail/tīwaiwaka or a banded rail/moho pererū. The episode can be sexually anatomical in traditional summaries; avoid treating this as an erotic scene or turning Hine-nui-te-pō into a disposable boss monster.

The tradition also connects Hine-tītama's movement to the realm of the dead with her response to knowledge of her own ancestry; her own agency must be acknowledged.

## 8. Trickster not saint

Some collected Māori accounts include cruelty—such as transforming his brother-in-law Irawaru into a dog—and tragic repercussions. Be careful not to present Māui as always morally flawless. The myths are layered; an event can be a comic trick, a warning and an explanation for the world simultaneously. The Disney redemption arc is a *different* character path.

Source: https://teara.govt.nz/en/1966/maui-legends-of/page-5

## Story application

Do: ask 'whose help is invisible?', 'what price does a trick impose?', 'whose authority is dismissed?', 'can Maui bear not being celebrated?'.
Do not: rewrite an iwi's origin story to resolve a fictional romance, copy ancestral karakia, or make Hine-nui-te-pō a recurring monster without cultural review.

In Lanternweald, place the **character's response** to the idea of time, care, mortality, being remembered and elder knowledge at the center rather than re-enacting a sacred episode on an unrelated stage.
