# Maui and Jasper: dialogue, relationships and scene craft

This file is an editorial writing guide. It is **not** a claim about the personal lives of actors, the intentions of traditional narrators, or an official Disney romance.

## Character backbone

- **Desire:** to belong even when no one is watching; he initially mistakes admiration for security.
- **Gift:** courage, invention, comic timing, quick action and growing capacity to protect without claiming.
- **Fault:** fills every silence; jokes before he understands; wants to make repair an immediate grand gesture.
- **Pressure point:** a competent small craftsperson asks him to wait his turn.
- **Private tenderness:** remembers a quiet preference and follows it without narrating the sacrifice.
- **Real stake:** being loved while not exceptional that day.

## Dialogue rhythms, original examples

Maui: 'I could carry the whole pier.'
Jasper: 'You could. That would make it a *mobile* pier. Fen wants a stable one.'
Maui: 'Fine. I'll carry the toolbox and receive absolutely no applause for it.'
Jasper: 'You can have a biscuit.'
Maui: 'Now we're negotiating.'

Later:
Maui: 'When they needed me, at least I knew what I was for.'
Jasper: 'You can stay without being useful.'
Maui: 'That is a terrifying offer.'
Jasper: 'I know.'
Maui: 'I want to try.'

These invented samples model the shift from performance to vulnerability; do not reproduce them as traditional Māori speech or Disney dialogue.

## Romantic pacing without flattening the character

- **Chemistry:** banter based on real differences in reasoning, not repeated dominance clichés.
- **Trust:** Maui learns Jasper's no is a boundary, not a challenge or a threat of abandonment.
- **Desire:** can coexist with the need to obtain a dock permit, cook supper, or finish a dangerous weather check.
- **Jealousy:** he may feel it and request reassurance, but cannot secretly redirect Jasper's relationships or choices.
- **Conflict repair:** specificity: 'I embarrassed you by making the welcome about me,' followed by changed conduct.
- **Intimacy:** sensual build-up, earned mutual choice, scene-specific fade-to-black, tender and sometimes awkward aftermath.
- **Non-exclusivity:** Jasper says plainly that they are ethically non-monogamous and prefer polyamory. Maui gets to negotiate his own capacities and boundaries without claiming everyone else's calendar.
- **Other partners:** remain named human beings and independent off-page lives. This novel does not annex them as scenery.

## Seven different modes of humor

1. **Scale joke:** he imagines a titanic solution to a tiny problem.
2. **Procedural joke:** the hero loses an argument to a clipboard and tide table.
3. **Body comedy:** a bird-shift goes wrong at precisely the worst social moment (Disney continuity).
4. **Self-awareness:** Mini Maui silently judges his latest boast (animated-film device).
5. **Deadpan from Jasper:** they calmly propose an outrageously practical alternative.
6. **Gentle rival banter:** Greg, Iroh or another visitor cuts through his need for an audience.
7. **After an apology:** he risks one small joke to see whether a relationship has recovered; lets it go if it hasn't.

## Places as cast members

**Tideglass Cove:** salt-stiff rope, pier boards, tide glass, repaired signs, wet sailcloth, fish records, people working.
**Lanternwell Square:** a noticeboard that needs accurate times, privacy protections, painters, a bell.
**Moonroot Archive:** ledgers with uncertainties and consent rules, not a magical spoiler machine.
**Starwake Pier:** bookable sound gear, paying workers, safe stage loads.
**Brasswatch Yard:** limits, tested materials, accessible latches, safety checks.
**Jasmine Steps / Riverstone Terrace:** food, gardens, neighbors and their competing needs.

## Chapter QA rubric

Ask these ten questions when deepening an existing Maui chapter:
1. What changes materially by the end?
2. Who besides Jasper/Maui makes a decision with consequences?
3. How does the tide or district infrastructure affect the action?
4. Is this Maui's recognizable voice rather than generic romantic dialogue?
5. What specific thing has Jasper chosen or declined?
6. Has existing polyamory/consent continuity been respected?
7. Is any Māori detail accurately labelled and actually sourced?
8. Are traditional gods/creatures being used as decor or unearned antagonists?
9. Are setting and character facts consistent with chapters before and after?
10. Does the chapter close on a distinctive changed understanding or practical result instead of an abstract moral?

## Community consultant questions

For a culture-specific scene ask a knowledgeable Māori consultant about tribe/place specificity, attribution, consent to represent material, terminology, modern performance conditions, and whether the scene imposes an outsider explanatory burden. A consultant can challenge the scene's premise, not just correct spelling.
