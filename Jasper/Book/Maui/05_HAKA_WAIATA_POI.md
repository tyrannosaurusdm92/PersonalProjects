# Māori dance, performance, song and musical context

**No single 'Māori dance' exists.** Forms have distinct histories, purposes, composers, owners, settings, and contemporary uses. Haka is far more than a war dance; poi is not automatically a courtship ceremony; and waiata aroha often expresses longing, distress or separation, rather than simply celebrating a new romance.

## Haka: embodied communication

Kapa haka is a living collective performance practice. Depending on form and context, haka can express challenge, grief, welcome, joy, solidarity, memory, praise and political speech. Terms documented in Te Ara include:

- **Haka taparahi:** ceremonial haka without weapons.
- **Ngeri:** exhortation intended to stir a group into action; without weapons.
- **Tūtūngārahu / ngārahu:** historically a pre-battle demonstration or diagnostic dance with weapons in published descriptions.
- **Peruperu:** a particularly fierce battle-associated haka, traditionally performed with weapons.
- **Haka pōwhiri:** haka offered in welcoming contexts; forms and performers differ by iwi.
- **Wiri:** trembling/quivering hand movement with connections to traditions around Tānerore and shimmering summer air.

The correct form cannot be inferred solely from 'Maui feels angry.' Composer attribution and setting matter. It is possible to describe the *effect* of seeing a real performance without writing custom te reo Māori lyrics or inventing a false sacred choreography.

## Waiata: songs that hold history and relationships

| Form | General use in cited sources | Fiction possibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Waiata aroha** | Love, longing, separation, mourning connections | A character talks about why longing is not evidence of ownership. |
| **Waiata tangi** | Laments, loss, illness, death, separation and grief | A scene can respect silence and the performer, not demand a comforting show. |
| **Oriori** | Genealogy and histories communicated to children | A character learns that education is carried by song, without copying family whakapapa. |
| **Ruri** | Amorous song forms | Avoid claiming every ruri is a flirtatious group dance. |
| **Pātere** | Often rapid and defiant responses to insult, jealousy or slander | A performer has their own social stake and their own voice. |
| **Pao** | Short sung or chanted material | Modern original artists may use topical material with attribution. |
| **Waiata whaiāipo** | Bold, playful, witty flirtatious songs described in scholarship | A nuanced reference for romance, never a universal dating rite. |
| **Waiata poi** | Poi movements accompanying song and often symbolic journeys | A choreographer controls their own performance. |

Source: https://teara.govt.nz/en/traditional-maori-songs-waiata-tawhito/page-3

## Poi: art and object

Poi is an artform and a taonga: traditionally a weighted ball and cord, with uses historically spanning music, health, martial training, narrative and recreation. Modern poi frequently appears in kapa haka. It is not historically restricted to romantic situations, nor always a standalone 'dance.' Materials and rhythms carry different traditions.

Te Papa: https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/digital-museum/explore-digital-museum/poi

## Whare tapere and performance spaces

The whare tapere is described in research as a traditional house or occasion for recreation, including stories, songs, movement, games, instruments and some ancestral love stories. This is a much more faithful avenue to research arts and romantic storytelling than inventing a generic sacred mating ritual.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/leisure-in-traditional-maori-society-nga-mahi-a-te-rehia/page-4

## Taonga pūoro: music made of relationships

Kōauau are flutes; pūtōrino has associations with Raukatauri and the case moth; pūtātara and pūkāea are signaling/trumpet instruments; porotiti and pūrerehua are spun objects with varied sound and healing associations. Instruments have stories, materials and individual voices. Avoid implying one flute universally summons gods or can be played by anyone in any ritual context.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/maori-musical-instruments-taonga-puoro

## How to write a good scene

1. Establish **who composed/organized** the event, where it is performed and who invited whom.
2. Let the organizer speak about the performance's **specific contemporary purpose**, not give an encyclopedic lesson.
3. Make space for logistics: rehearsals, disabled access, refreshments, payment, time, consent for recording and sound.
4. Describe what Jasper *hears and feels* without pretending to translate material they do not know.
5. Afterward, Maui and Jasper may share their **own private invented dance**. Call it their dance, not a traditional Māori courtship rite.
6. If a named real traditional composition, haka or iwi-specific performance appears, verify its provenance and obtain permission where appropriate.

## Existing story fit

The existing The Shore I Choose chapters 6 and 25 include Mereana, an **invented visiting Māori artist**. Keep her performance a contemporary piece made and led by her group and do not retrofit it into an ancient universal ritual. Her right to say no and to leave should persist. Further characterization can give her an artistic career, travel schedule, colleagues, fees, values, frustrations and creative goals separate from Maui's romance.

## Negative examples to avoid

- 'Maui performs the Māori mating haka to claim Jasper.'
- 'A random magical dance is sacred in every Polynesian island.'
- 'Poi is only a women's romantic dance' or 'haka is only performed by men in war.'
- Inventing untranslated pseudo-Māori chants or misusing publicly shared karakia as spell components.

The fix is not removing culture from the fiction; it is specificity, research, attribution and character agency.
