# Maui across Polynesia: related stories, different traditions

## The comparison problem

Māui/Maui narratives appear across a large Polynesian world. Shared themes reflect ancestral connections and long oral histories, but each community's version has its own genealogy, geography, moral framing and living custodians. Do not refer to Hawaiian stories as Māori, or to Māori gods as a single set of gods for all Polynesia.

A useful method for every scene: identify **which tradition**, **which source or storyteller**, **which version**, and whether the event is **film fiction**, **traditional account**, or **new fiction**.

## Comparisons worth recording

| Motif | Māori / Aotearoa | Hawaiian / Hawaiʻi | Writing caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Island/fish feat | Māui fishes up Te Ika-a-Māui, identified with the North Island; Te Waka-a-Māui represents the canoe in a South Island naming tradition. | Some narratives describe Māui trying to pull separated Hawaiian islands together rather than literally creating them from the sea. University of Hawaiʻi geology teaching notes explicitly warn against blending these versions. | Don't make every island legend identical. |
| Magical hook | Māori sources describe the jawbone of his ancestress Murirangawhenua. | Mānaiakalani / Manaiakalani is a named fishhook in Hawaiian cultural and star traditions. | The movie's carved hook and its transformation mechanics are a separate visual canon. |
| Family | Taranga as mother, Makeatutara as father in several Māori accounts; his elder brothers share variations of the name. | Hina is named as Maui's mother in some Hawaiian stories. | Do not assign Hina as his Māori mother without tracing a specific source. |
| Sun | Ropes and the jawbone allow Māui and brothers to slow Tamanuiterā. | Retellings also feature capturing/slowing the sun, with variant materials and kinship. | Shared feat does not mean every line, tool and prayer is common. |
| Fire | Mahuika and her nails, the fire in certain trees, a disastrous act of trickery. | Fire-making myths occur in other Pacific narrative clusters with distinct elders and protagonists. | Do not invent a generic 'fire goddess' identical everywhere. |
| Death | An attempt to overcome Hine-nui-te-pō fails and Māui dies in many Māori accounts. | Endings and emphasis vary elsewhere. | Film Maui's survival should not erase or 'correct' Māori tradition. |

## Specific Hawaiian caution: the islands

University of Hawaiʻi's geological-traditions reading discusses Maui's attempted **joining** of the Hawaiian islands, distinguished from the account that Papa and Wākea created them. One variant has the pulling line snap after a helper looks away. This is valuable as a documented **difference**, not an invitation to edit the Māori North Island tale.

University of Hawaiʻi's Hawaiian star-names resource relates Mānaiakalani to a star family and the asterism of Maui's hook; this can inspire an original story about navigation **without** transplanting Hawaiian celestial naming into a Māori ceremony.

## Other Pacific tellings

Pacific stories from Samoa, Tonga, the Society Islands, the Cook Islands and beyond include heroic trickery, fire, islands, sky and solar feats; not all contain each episode in the same way. For a specific Samoan or Tongan narrative, **obtain and attribute a story published by storytellers/scholars of that community** before describing family, sacred speech, deity names or what 'everyone' believes. Avoid relying exclusively on a synthesized tourist retelling.

## What counts as respectful creative transformation?

- A fictional sea passage can echo the *theme* of making time for others without staging Māui's sun myth as if original.
- An argument with a sibling can explore the cost of trickery without impersonating a documented ancestral family member.
- If a Hawaiian character shares an account of Mānaiakalani, identify their version; another character may know a Māori jawbone tradition but would not casually announce one 'correct' version.
- Never treat a modern Indigenous performer or storyteller as free cultural exposition for protagonists; they should have a life, permissions and motivations of their own.
- Geographic distinctions matter: Aotearoa is not Hawaiʻi; 'Māori', 'Native Hawaiian', 'Samoan', and 'Tongan' are not stylistic adjectives to swap at will.

## Research confidence

High for the Māori feat names and Hawaiian *joining versus creation* distinction documented in sources above. Lower for any exact pan-Polynesian genealogy: record variants as specific, not universal. Old printed archives should be read as historical collected accounts, not automatically as community-approved definitive versions.
