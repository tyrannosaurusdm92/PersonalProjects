# Tikanga, welcome, ritual, spiritual concepts and boundaries

## Context first

**Tikanga** broadly refers to appropriate ways of doing things; details can differ among iwi, hapū, marae, occasion, participants and contemporary community. This file is a **recognition guide**, not an instruction manual for staging a ritual. **Kawa** names more localized formal protocols in many marae contexts. A fictional community should not use an invented procedure while labeling it a real Māori custom.

## Pōwhiri: a relationship being established

Pōwhiri is a formal welcome, especially on a marae. Across many documented practices the phases may include:
- **Karanga:** formal calls, establishing welcome and connections.
- **Wero:** a challenge in some formal welcomes; not universal.
- **Whaikōrero:** formal speeches by appropriate speakers according to local kawa.
- **Waiata:** songs supporting speeches.
- **Koha:** contribution or gift from visitors.
- **Hongi and harirū:** ceremonial greeting and handshake, often after formal speeches.
- **Hākari:** shared meal.
- **Poroporoaki:** formal leave-taking when appropriate.

The participants, order, speaking roles, and meanings vary; the public outline is **not** permission to improvise a pōwhiri for a fantasy wedding or to commandeer someone's marae. Te Ara notes links between the space of the meeting-house courtyard, Tūmatauenga, and the interior associated with Rongo.

## Karakia and tohunga

**Karakia** encompass invocations, prayers, ritual formulae and other speech practices across traditional and modern contexts. **Tohunga** are specialists, sometimes ritual experts and sometimes expert craftworkers. A made-up chant written in plausible-looking Māori is not authentic karakia. Do not invent restricted rituals, pretend they are universal, or stage a healer as an exotic magic vending machine.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/traditional-maori-religion-nga-karakia-a-te-maori

## A working conceptual glossary

- **Mana:** authority, influence, prestige, spiritual potency and the obligations that go with them; not an RPG mana meter.
- **Tapu:** state of sacredness/restriction and relational protection; not just 'cursed'.
- **Noa:** ordinary, free from particular restrictions; can be part of transitions after a ritual.
- **Mauri:** life principle / vital essence associated with people, places, waters, forests and more; not generic hit points.
- **Wairua:** spiritual aspect, often translated as spirit; context matters.
- **Rāhui:** temporary restriction or prohibition, including care for resources and sites.
- **Kaitiakitanga:** guardianship/stewardship with relational responsibility and authority.
- **Whakapapa:** genealogical and layered relational connections, not a collectible database of deities.
- **Tangata whenua:** people of the land, tied to specific place and authority, not simply anyone who arrived first.

## Death, mourning and memory

**Tangihanga** is a complex living practice surrounding death, gathering, grief and farewell. Hine-nui-te-pō's story can inform the cosmological background, but a funeral is not a dramatic prop for Maui's regret. If representing a real Māori tangi, research community-specific settings and involve advisors. A Lanternweald remembrance meal should be invented and described as **Lanternweald practice**, not relabelled a tangihanga.

Te Ara: https://teara.govt.nz/en/tangihanga-death-customs/print

## Tattoos and bodily sovereignty

**Tā moko** is not just a pattern library. Māori moko has history, art practice, identity, lineage, authority and living practitioners; Te Papa discusses Niwareka and Mataora in the origin story. Other Polynesian tattooing traditions are **not interchangeable** with tā moko. Disney's Maui uses a stylized animated tattoo record; avoid copying recognizable moko into a character design, a ritual, or cover art without engagement and permission.

## A fictional encounter checklist

Before including a Māori ceremonial setting ask:
1. Where are we? Whose marae or community? Who is hosting?
2. Who invited Jasper and Maui? What do they know, and what do they need to learn?
3. Is the event contemporary, historical, or imagined?
4. Are language, composition, performance and genealogy attributed?
5. Who is permitted to speak or act, according to the *specific* hosts?
6. Can a person refuse photography, performance or emotional labor?
7. Are safety, accessibility and travel arrangements actually discussed?
8. Would a Māori advisor reject this as stitched-together pan-Pacific spectacle?

**Safer story decision:** represent a host explaining that the protagonist must follow their lead, or move the scene to a clearly invented Lanternweald festival with no false ethnographic label.
