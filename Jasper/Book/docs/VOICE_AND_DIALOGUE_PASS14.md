# Pass 14 — Dialogue, Voice and Intimacy Guide for the Six Fanfiction Novels

**Purpose:** a practical editing guide based on the repository's `json/william-writing-style.json` fingerprint and the current six-manuscript canon. It is a craft aid, **not** evidence that every scene perfectly reproduces an individual writer's voice. Don't copy passages from the reference corpus verbatim.

## Jasper's first-person narration

Jasper narrates immediate choices and limited observations, not omniscient explanations. A scene often moves from a physical detail (a wet sleeve, badly labeled bin, crooked chair, humming kettle) to a funny exchange and only then to a private fear. Keep the interior thought short when the moment is acute. Leave longer reflection for silence after an exchange or after somebody leaves. The existing style reference emphasizes emotionally direct prose, sensory anchors, alternating short and long paragraphs, and dialogue interrupted by gestures rather than speeches.

Jasper is **an adult**, and externally uses they/them. They explicitly state **ethical non-monogamy** and a preference for **polyamory** to romantic partners. Each love may be a real, independent relationship rather than an enforced single configuration. Retain planned time, truthful disclosure and respect for what other partners have not consented to make public. Jasper may be nervous, desire touch, miss someone, say no, make a poor repair, overpromise or lose patience; a "healthy relationship" should not make them superhumanly articulate.

**Do not flatten the voices:** "You can tell me what you need" is not a universal incantation to put in everyone's mouth. Maui might make a bad joke first, Toph might call the plan stupid, Severus might correct an imprecise verb, Iroh might change a tea blend, Danse might ask for a dimension, and Greg might accidentally hum the answer.

## Building actual depth

1. **Place a stake in the room.** A named person needs a ride, a paid hour, a working latch or a private answer. Romantic stakes matter more when some other need survives the scene.
2. **Let the first attempt go a little wrong.** A joke lands badly; a route is inaccessible; a receipt is misdated. The mistake must belong to this scene, not a generic "communication issue".
3. **Differentiate who holds authority.** A resident's right to refuse remains real even when the protagonist and a famous partner mean well.
4. **Make a choice cost something small but tangible.** An altered booking, extra supply line, delayed performance, repaired cuff, or admitted fear.
5. **Stop before a summary recites the moral.** End on a surviving object or altered routine when possible: a key placed on a hook, a paid invoice, a corrected sign, a quiet bench with no scheduled performer.

## Romance, sensuality and fade-to-black

Flirtation can be clumsy. Someone may knock a bowl sideways while trying to kiss, reach for a hand and lose nerve, or look at another person's mouth and forget which sentence they were speaking. The physical detail is more affecting than repeated abstract reassurance. A scene can be warm or sensual without being explicit. When adults choose privacy, close the door naturally after the emotional choice; do not write explicit sexual acts or treat intimacy as the reward for doing civic work.

Retain the **first** significant romantic turning point of each existing novel rather than replacing it with a formulaic scene. Later private encounters should have different emotional purposes—reunion, apology, grief, desire, nervous laughter, quiet companionship—and can fade to black instead of repeating the initial moment.

## Negative-pattern checklist before a commit

- Does the chapter contain two goodnight scenes or an abrupt next morning followed by another night?
- Do two partners deliver the same eloquent consent speech with only their names changed?
- Is a crossover only a celebrity appearing to nod at Jasper?
- Did someone resolve a historic injustice because a resident accepted tea?
- Does a new intimacy scene override a previous boundary, or pretend all partners are automatically involved with one another?
- Is the "new world's magic" merely a shop that gives free labor and endless resources?
- Is a distinctive local disaster or character history contradicted elsewhere?
- Did the ending restate the same theme four times, when one physical detail would carry it better?
- Did we change source chapter JSON without updating `series.json` and the offline reader overlay?

The pass-14 Iroh chapter 21 rewrite demonstrates the chosen approach: a spare key becomes a real conflict about a chosen home and paid shifts, interrupted by Mina's expertise, a damaged stock jar, and a stranger's memory of the war. The romance grows from those distinct choices, not from an explanatory paragraph telling the reader the relationship is healthy. Iroh chapter 20 and Greg chapter 19 had redundant or contradictory endings cut in the same pass.

**Quality claims are editorial, not automated tests.** Word-count parity is already complete; the remaining goal is stronger, more individual scenes.
